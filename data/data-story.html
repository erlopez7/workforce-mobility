Model 1 Used in Final Platform — Job Automation Risk

Method

Rule-based interpretable scoring on 1,016 O*NET occupations.

Inputs: routine tasks, nonroutine tasks, social tasks, manual tasks, job zone.

Output + Result

Risk score (0–4) and risk label (Low / Moderate / High).

Final split: 446 Low | 314 Moderate | 256 High.

Steer with your eyes open: most jobs are not the same kind of “safe” when work gets automated

WorkForce scores every O*NET occupation in our universe (1,016 roles) using a transparent, rule-based model from task data: routine vs. nonroutine cognitive work, social and manual tasks, and job zone. The chart below shows how many occupations land in each risk band. That matters for you because labels are a planning lens—not a verdict on your worth—so you can compare paths, pair training, and ask better questions in Career Paths or the AI Advisor.

Low automation-style risk

Roles that lean on judgment, nonroutine thinking, social interaction, and/or higher job zones in our task mix. In this model they are relatively less exposed to work that is easy to substitute with software or streamlined processes—often a stronger starting point when you want staying power as tools and hiring change.

Moderate automation-style risk

A mixed profile: some tasks could be compressed by tools or workflow change, alongside work that is harder to codify. Reasonable paths if you pair the role with a plan—transferable skills, a clear next-step occupation, or credentials that open adjacent doors.

High automation-style risk

In our scoring, these occupations sit toward the more routinizable or substitutable end of the task ladder. That is not “do not do this job”—many people use these roles as on-ramps. It does mean: know the tradeoff, budget time to level up or pivot, and use the platform to spot lower-exposure neighbors you can grow into.

Reading the bars: Each bar is how many occupations earned that label after the full scoring run. A wide footprint across all three bands shows the real economy is mixed—and why personalized recommendations (Model 2) sit on top of this foundation instead of a one-size-fits-all job list.

For decisions: Use Low / Moderate / High to compare options side by side, not to rank people. Prefer Low when you want the model’s read on longer-run task resilience; treat High as a signal to plan the next credential or adjacent role; use Moderate when you are balancing speed-to-work with a backup skill. Labels come from public O*NET task structure in our rules—your coach, program, or local labor market still matter.

What “disadvantaged” and “advantaged” mean here — and how the four groups differ

      
People often read these labels as judgments about a student. In WorkForce they are not personal grades. They summarize typical neighborhood conditions (census tract averages) after we cluster 73,202 tracts on poverty, upward mobility estimates, median income, and opportunity gap—using KNN-imputed values where mobility was missing. Your ZIP (or tract, in a full deployment) is a shortcut to “which neighborhood bucket looks most like mine for planning tools,” not “who I am.”


      

        

          
“Disadvantaged” side (harder average conditions)

          
Very Disadvantaged and Disadvantaged are the two groups with higher poverty and lower upward-mobility signals in our summary table than the two opportunity groups. Very Disadvantaged is the more extreme of the two on poverty and mobility.

        

        

          
“More opportunity” side (stronger average conditions)

          
Moderate Opportunity and Higher Opportunity are the two groups with lower poverty and higher incomes in the platform rollup. Higher Opportunity also has the highest mobility score (0.51 vs 0.41 for the middle two).

        

      


      
Why “Disadvantaged” and “Moderate Opportunity” both show 0.41 mobility: In this public summary row, average upward mobility is the same for those two labels, but they are not duplicates. Poverty and median income differ a lot (see the table). The cluster model separates tracts using the full feature mix; the table highlights a few averages so you can compare bands without reading the full methodology.


      
Jobs vs neighborhoods: Automation risk labels apply to occupations (the same 1,016 jobs exist for everyone). Opportunity groups apply to place context—they change education ROI defaults and which short ranked list we surface in the demo CSV, not the underlying job-risk math.

Why one career shortlist cannot fit every neighborhood

      
WorkForce does not give every ZIP the same ranked jobs on purpose. Opportunity groups summarize different average neighborhood conditions (poverty, mobility signals, income, education cost context). Model 2 still uses the same occupation definitions and Model 1 automation-risk scores everywhere—but it re-ranks candidates for each group using accessibility, risk, and match weights tuned to that context. So the lists are not identical for three straightforward reasons: (1) inputs differ—each column is a different tract “profile,” not the same student; (2) ordering differs—a job that is a strong early pick for one context may slide for another when tradeoffs shift; (3) membership differs—some roles only appear in certain groups’ top-fifteen exports (see the dashes). The heatmap makes that visible: same row (job), different colors across columns (priority by group).

      

        
What the cell number is (and what it is not). There is no separate “opportunity rank” score for a job. The column is the neighborhood opportunity group (Very Disadvantaged, Disadvantaged, Moderate, Higher). The number in the cell is only that job’s position on that group’s Model 2 shortlist: 1 = first suggestion for that group, 15 = fifteenth. It is not a national ranking of “how good the opportunity is” in the abstract—it is “where this job landed when we sorted the list for tracts like this.”

        
Why those positions change across columns. The model blends automation risk, training accessibility (job zone), and match-to-context. For disadvantaged-area profiles, lower job-zone roles (supervision, landscaping, childcare) often rise to the top of the export; for moderate/higher profiles, the same pipeline can prioritize different mixes (e.g. more job-zone-3 tech and operations roles). Same occupations, same risk labels from Model 1—different sort order and sometimes different top-15 membership.

        
Example from the bundled recommendations file. Web Developers does not appear in the Very Disadvantaged or Disadvantaged top-15 at all (those cells are “—”), but it is #4 for both Moderate Opportunity and Higher Opportunity. Hydroelectric Production Managers is #5 for Very Disadvantaged and Disadvantaged, yet #1 for Moderate and Higher. Landscaping and Groundskeeping Workers is #3 for Very Disadvantaged and Disadvantaged but is not in the Moderate/Higher top-15 here—so one neighborhood context’s “near the top” job can be absent from another’s shortlist entirely.

Imputed mobility — missingness before vs after KNN

      
KMeans uses tract-level pov20, mob_up, inc20, and opp_gap. Raw merges left many tracts without upward mobility; KNN imputation fills those features so clustering and downstream UI logic do not drop rows or fail on nulls.

      

        
      

      

      

        Key insight: Upward mobility missingness drops from about 16% to 0% after KNN imputation, allowing all 73,202 tracts to remain usable in the clustering pipeline. These values are estimated, not originally observed.
